A summary of the history of murals in Rotterdam

After the bombing of 14 May 1940, the city center was devastated. Reconstruction changed the urban fabric, producing new public spaces.

Later, during the 1960s, contemporary art spread internationally. Walls became places for unofficial expression, and street art introduced new visual languages. The city’s working-class neighborhoods and multicultural population provided a rich urban setting for these developments.

During the 1990s, murals and graffiti became more and more visible as part of the city’s urban culture. Artists used walls to explore identity and neighborhood pride. Commissioned projects also began to offer more opportunities for large-scale painting.

The 2010s brought greater visibility to large-scale murals. They were being supported by artists, organizations, initiatives and regeneration projects. Murals also became a way to attract visitors.

Today, murals are a part of Rotterdam’s cultural landscape. Some are spectacular landmarks, while others tell stories about local communities, historical events or social issues.
